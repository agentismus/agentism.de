# Agentism design language

This document records the ideas behind the Agentism site: what each element means, where it comes from, and the rules for using it. Read it before changing copy, colour, type or layout. Section 10 says which historical claims and quotations have been checked against sources and which have not.

## 1. The idea in one paragraph

The site is set like an entry in a lexicon with notes in the margin. The main text is plain German business copy. The margin notes say where the words come from. The vocabulary of AI (Agent, Algorithmus, Automat, Potenzial) already carries a history that runs from Greek philosophy through the Islamic world into Latin and German, so the ties to Islamic civilization do not have to be added: they are in the words a German visitor already uses. Ashʿarī kalām supplies the sharper points: what an agent is, what a trained model knows, and who stays responsible.

## 2. Rules that keep it unforced

1. **Main text first.** A visitor who reads only the main column must understand what Agentism does and how to get in touch. Nothing essential lives in a margin note.
2. **Depth lives in the margin.** Each note carries one idea, stays under about 70 words, and connects to the business claim beside it. A note that does not connect is cut.
3. **Scripts only where a dictionary puts them.** Arabic and Greek appear in etymology brackets and margin notes, as single terms or short phrases, always with transliteration and a German meaning. Never whole sentences, never as decoration.
4. **Greek never stands alone.** Every Greek term appears with its Arabic continuation. The Greek material may be prominent but must not outweigh the Islamic material.
5. **Borrow openly.** When a concept from theology is applied to software or to a business question, the text says that it is borrowed (for example "Die Dreiteilung ist geliehen"). The original meaning is stated correctly.
6. **Mark interpretations.** Contested readings are attributed ("Yasser Tabbaa liest die Form als …"), never stated as fact.
7. **No religious text as ornament.** No Qurʾānic text, no divine names, no calligraphy used for looks. No crescents, lamps or arabesque borders.
8. **Sources or silence.** A historical claim goes on the site only if it is recorded in section 4 with its source. Quotation marks are used only for wording that has been checked (section 10).
9. **Red is for headwords.** Red marks headwords, numerals and key terms, as rubrication does in manuscripts. It is never a background or a button colour.

## 3. The word

**Agentism** = _agent_ + _-ism_.

- _agent_ comes from Latin _agēns_ ("acting, doing"), the present participle of _agere_ ("to drive, do, act"). _Agere_ is cognate with Greek ἄγειν (_ágein_, "to lead"). German words from the same root: Agenda, Agens, Agent, Agentur, agieren, agil, Akt, Akteur, Aktion.
- _-ism_ comes from Greek -ισμός through Latin _-ismus_: a doctrine, stance or practice.
- The Arabic counterpart of _agēns_ is فاعل _fāʿil_ ("the one who acts"), from فعل _fiʿl_ ("act"). It is a counterpart, not an etymon, so it is introduced with "vgl." (compare).

The hero is a dictionary entry for the word, with four senses: the doer (philosophy), the representative (law), the software agent (computing), and the firm. The old headline "Wir bauen Systeme, die wirklich funktionieren." survives as the entry's usage example. The running head above the entry lists the dictionary neighbours from the same root.

## 4. The ties

Each entry gives the content, where it is used on the site, and how to phrase it.

### 4.1 The agent intellect

- **Content.** Aristotle (_De anima_ III.5) describes an intellect that "makes" all things intelligible. The commentators named it νοῦς ποιητικός. In Arabic philosophy it became العقل الفعّال _al-ʿaql al-faʿʿāl_ (al-Fārābī, d. 950; Ibn Sīnā, d. 1037; Ibn Rushd, d. 1198). With the translations from Arabic in the 12th and 13th centuries it reached the Latin schools as _intellectus agens_, the "agent intellect", and was taken up by Albertus Magnus in Cologne (d. 1280) and Thomas Aquinas. German: _tätiger Verstand_ or _wirkende Vernunft_.
- **What it does.** It turns what is potentially known into what is actually known.
- **Why it matters here.** The first thing in European languages to be called an "agent" intelligence got its doctrine through Arabic. The Latin _agens_ in _intellectus agens_ is the same word as in _Agent_.
- **On the site.** Margin note in "Arbeitsweise" (keyword _tätig_). Sense 1 of the hero entry. "Abb. 2" in the same section draws the journey as five stations: Athens (Aristotle), Baghdad (al-Fārābī), Toledo (the translators from Arabic), Cologne (Albertus Magnus), Berlin (today).
- **Phrasing.** Say that the concept travelled through Arabic philosophy. Do not say that the everyday word "Agent" was borrowed from Arabic.

### 4.2 Potency and act

- **Content.** Aristotle's pair δύναμις / ἐνέργεια became قوّة _quwwa_ / فعل _fiʿl_ in Arabic and _potentia_ / _actus_ in Latin. German: Potenz and Akt, or Möglichkeit and Wirklichkeit. The consultant's word "Potenzial" is this _potentia_.
- **On the site.** The process section is titled "Vom Möglichen zum Wirklichen". Margin note on "Potenzial" beside the KI-Potenzialanalyse.

### 4.3 Who is the agent? _Fāʿil_, _kasb_ and al-Rāzī

- **Content.** The question of who truly acts is central to kalām. The Ashʿarī answer is _kasb_ (كسب, "acquisition"): God creates the act and the human acquires it. Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī (d. 1210) put the human situation in a formula: the human being is مضطرّ في صورة مختار _muḍṭarr fī ṣūrat mukhtār_, "compelled in the form of one who chooses".
- **Why it matters here.** For humans the formula is contested. For a software agent it is a literal description: it "chooses" what its weights and its instructions determine. The practical consequence is that responsibility stays with a person.
- **On the site.** Margin note beside "KI-Agenten" (keyword _Wahl_).
- **Phrasing.** Always say that the sentence was about humans and that this is disputed. Apply it to machines only.

### 4.4 Al-Sanūsī: three judgements of reason

- **Content.** Muḥammad b. Yūsuf al-Sanūsī (d. 1490, Tlemcen) opens his creed _Umm al-Barāhīn_ by saying that rational judgement is confined to three: necessity, impossibility and possibility (الوجوب، الاستحالة، الجواز). The necessary (واجب _wājib_) is what reason cannot conceive as not existing. The impossible (مستحيل _mustaḥīl_) is what reason cannot conceive as existing. The possible (جائز _jāʾiz_) is what reason accepts as existing or not existing.
- **On the site.** The section "Drei Urteile, bevor wir bauen" sorts every project idea into notwendig, unmöglich or möglich. This gives the firm's promise ("we say where AI does not work") a structure.
- **Phrasing.** The margin note says the division is borrowed and that al-Sanūsī meant something greater. In his text the three judgements serve the proof of what is necessary, impossible and possible regarding God. The site borrows only the division.

### 4.5 Al-Sanūsī: judgement from habit (_ḥukm ʿādī_)

- **Content.** Al-Sanūsī distinguishes legal, rational and habitual judgement. The habitual judgement (الحكم العادي _al-ḥukm al-ʿādī_) affirms a link between two things because they were repeatedly found together. The exception remains possible, and neither thing has any effect on the other. This is the Ashʿarī position on causation (compare al-Ghazālī, _Tahāfut al-falāsifa_, discussion 17).
- **Why it matters here.** It describes what a trained model has: associations formed by repetition in its data, without necessity. A model knows what usually follows, not what must follow.
- **On the site.** Margin note in "Einwände", beside the objection that language models make things up (keyword _Gewohnheit_).
- **Phrasing.** Paraphrase only, without quotation marks, until the Arabic wording has been checked against an edition (section 10).

### 4.6 The deciding reason (_murajjiḥ_)

- **Content.** In kalām, what can either be or not be needs a deciding factor (مرجِّح _murajjiḥ_) to come into existence. Al-Rāzī uses the principle throughout his work.
- **On the site.** Margin note beside the third judgement ("möglich"): a possible project needs a reason that tips the balance, and in a project that reason is measurable benefit.

### 4.7 Agency in law: _wakāla_ and Vollmacht

- **Content.** Islamic law treats acting on behalf of another as وكالة _wakāla_. The agent (وكيل _wakīl_) acts within the mandate given by the principal (_muwakkil_). An act outside the mandate is that of an unauthorised agent (_fuḍūlī_); in Ḥanafī and Mālikī law it is suspended (_mawqūf_) until the principal ratifies it (_ijāza_). German law has the same structure: Stellvertretung (§§ 164 ff. BGB), Vollmacht (§ 167 BGB), and the contract of an unauthorised representative, which is _schwebend unwirksam_ until approved (§ 177 BGB).
- **Why it matters here.** It is the second meaning of "agent", and it maps directly onto agent permissions and human approval.
- **On the site.** Sense 2 of the hero entry. Margin note in "Einwände", beside the objection about autonomous agents (keyword _Vollmacht_).
- **Caution.** _Al-Wakīl_ is also a divine name. Use the legal term _wakāla_ only, never the name, and never as a brand element.

### 4.8 Chains of sources (_isnād_)

- **Content.** The scholars of ḥadīth required for every report the chain of people who transmitted it (إسناد _isnād_). ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Mubārak (d. 797) gave the reason: without the isnād, anyone could say whatever he wished. The saying is recorded in the introduction to Muslim's _Ṣaḥīḥ_.
- **Why it matters here.** It is the oldest statement of the problem that retrieval with citations solves.
- **On the site.** Margin note beside "RAG-Systeme" (keyword _Quelle_).
- **Phrasing.** Quote only the second half of the saying. The first half ("the isnād is part of religion") is a religious statement and does not belong in a note about software.
- **Note.** This tie was added during the build. It was not in the first brainstorm.

### 4.9 Ingenious devices: _ḥiyal_, automata, algorithm

- **Content.** The Arabic continuation of Greek mechanics (Philo of Byzantium, Hero of Alexandria) was called علم الحيل _ʿilm al-ḥiyal_, the science of ingenious devices. The Banū Mūsā brothers (Baghdad, 9th century) wrote the _Kitāb al-Ḥiyal_ (c. 850) and described an "instrument that plays by itself": a flute player whose melody was set by pins on a rotating cylinder, often called the first programmable machine. Al-Jazarī completed his _Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices_ in 1206 at the Artuqid (Turkmen) court in Diyarbakır; it includes automaton musicians driven by a pegged drum. "Algorithmus" is the Latinised name of al-Khwārizmī (d. c. 850); "Algebra" comes from _al-jabr_ in the title of his book; "Ziffer" and "Chiffre" come from _ṣifr_. "Automat" is Greek αὐτόματον, "self-moving".
- **On the site.** Margin note beside "Integration & Automatisierung" (keyword _Automat_).

### 4.10 The chess automaton (cautionary only)

- **Content.** In 1770 Wolfgang von Kempelen showed a chess-playing "automaton" at the Viennese court: a figure in Ottoman costume at a cabinet. A chess master sat inside. Walter Benjamin opens his theses "Über den Begriff der Geschichte" (1940) with it: the puppet always wins because a hidden dwarf, theology, guides its hand.
- **Why it is not an homage.** The Ottoman link is only the costume. The object is a Habsburg court hoax and the standard emblem of fake AI.
- **On the site.** Margin note beside "KI-Sicherheitsaudit" (keyword _Kasten_): an audit opens the cabinet.
- **Rules.** Tell it as a story. Never use it as an emblem, an image or a slogan. Avoid the idioms "getürkt" and "einen Türken bauen", which are derogatory.
- **Private meaning.** Benjamin's image fits this site: its theology sits in the margins and guides the hand.

### 4.11 Definitions: the dictionary form

- **Content.** Books of definitions are a genre of Islamic scholarship: al-Kindī's _On the Definitions and Descriptions of Things_, Ibn Sīnā's _Kitāb al-Ḥudūd_, and al-Sharīf al-Jurjānī's _Kitāb al-Taʿrīfāt_ (d. 1413). Defining a thing by genus and difference (حدّ _ḥadd_) comes from Aristotle (ὁρισμός). Aristotle's _Metaphysics_ Δ is itself a lexicon, and al-Fārābī's _Risāla fī l-ʿaql_ lists the senses in which "intellect" is said.
- **Why it matters here.** The dictionary entry is not only a Greek form. Definition is where Aristotle and kalām meet, which makes it the natural place for the two traditions to work together.
- **On the site.** The hero entry. Each section opens with a lemma line in dictionary form ("Urteil, das"). Margin note in "Über uns" (keyword _Begriff_).
- **Conventions.** Follow German dictionary practice: headword, article and grammar, etymology in angle brackets, subject labels in italics, numbered senses. The brackets are set as ‹ › because the typeface has no ⟨ ⟩.

### 4.12 Text and gloss: the page form

- **Content.** Islamic scholarship transmitted knowledge as a short core text (متن _matn_), its commentary (شرح _sharḥ_) and glosses in the margin (حاشية _ḥāshiya_). Al-Sanūsī's _Umm al-Barāhīn_ is among the most commented core texts of the Maghrib. European glossed manuscripts work the same way.
- **On the site.** Main column and margin column. On small screens the notes follow the paragraph they belong to.

### 4.13 Disputation: _in qīla … qulnā_

- **Content.** Kalām works are written against objections: فإن قيل … قلنا _fa-in qīla … qulnā_, "if it is said …, we say …". The rules of such debate were later set out as a discipline of its own (_ādāb al-baḥth wa-l-munāẓara_). Latin scholasticism has the same form (_videtur quod … respondeo_). Al-Rāzī was known for stating his opponents' objections at full strength. A well-known quip says he paid the objections in cash and the answers on credit.
- **On the site.** The section "Einwände" replaces an FAQ. Each item reads "Man wendet ein: … / Wir antworten: …". Margin note with the form and the quip (keyword _Einwand_).

### 4.14 The _zāʾirja_ (not on the site at present)

- **Content.** Ibn Khaldūn (_Muqaddima_, 1377) describes the زايرجة _zāʾirja_: a technique that derives an answer from the letters of a question. Many took it for knowledge of the unseen. His verdict is that the answer agrees with the question because it is obtained from its wording, and nothing more. It has been suggested that Ramon Llull knew the device and modelled his _Ars magna_ on it; Leibniz's _Dissertatio de arte combinatoria_ (1666) builds on Llull.
- **On the site.** Nowhere at present. It was written as the margin note for a chat section (keyword _Auskunft_), as an honest caveat about what a chat model's answers are. The chat was removed when the site became static. Use it again if a chat returns.
- **Caution.** The device itself was divination. It appears only through Ibn Khaldūn's criticism of it.

### 4.15 Muqarnas

- **Content.** Muqarnas (مقرنص) is a vault built from small cells in tiers. Builders worked from two-dimensional ground plans (the Topkapı scroll; the plate from Takht-i Sulaymān). Al-Kāshī gave rules for measuring muqarnas in his _Miftāḥ al-Ḥisāb_ (Samarkand, 1427). Silvia Harmsen's Heidelberg dissertation (2006) reconstructs muqarnas vaults from their plans by algorithm.
- **Meaning.** Yasser Tabbaa reads the muqarnas dome, which appears in Iraq in the late 11th century, as an image of Ashʿarī occasionalism: a continuous dome dissolved into smallest parts that are held together from moment to moment. This is one scholar's interpretation and it is debated. For this site the point is that a computer does the same thing: it renders the continuous through the discrete.
- **On the site.** One figure, "Abb. 1", beside the hero entry (below it on small screens). It starts as a ground plan and rises into a vault as the visitor scrolls. The view is the classic one: looking up into the dome, slightly off its axis. It is computed in the browser from a rule, not drawn or loaded as a model. The plan follows the type of the early conical muqarnas domes of Iraq and Syria: concentric rings of points, each ring turned half a step against the one below, each cell standing on two neighbouring points and reaching to the point between them on the next ring.
- **Rules.** One figure, not wallpaper. Lines in the accent colour on the page ground. No photographs of buildings.

### 4.16 Ornament: the eight-pointed star

- **Content.** Girih patterns are built with compass and straightedge. Lu and Steinhardt (2007) showed that some 15th-century girih tilings are quasi-crystalline.
- **On the site.** A small eight-pointed star marks section breaks and serves as the favicon. It shares the eightfold and sixteenfold geometry of the muqarnas figure. Nothing more elaborate.

### 4.17 Colophon

- **Content.** Manuscripts end with a colophon that names how and when the book was made. "Kolophon" is Greek κολοφών, "summit, finishing touch".
- **On the site.** A short closing section explains the form of the page, the vault, the typefaces and the colours, links to the site's public source code, quotes Goethe, and dates the page in both calendars.

### 4.18 Bridges a German reader already has

- Goethe, _West-östlicher Divan_, from the poems added from his papers: "Wer sich selbst und andre kennt, / Wird auch hier erkennen: / Orient und Okzident / Sind nicht mehr zu trennen."
- Albertus Magnus in Cologne and the _intellectus agens_ (4.1).
- The BGB parallel to _wakāla_ (4.7).
- Everyday words from Arabic and Persian: Algorithmus, Algebra, Ziffer, Chiffre, Schachmatt, Azur, Karmesin.

### 5.1 Colour

| Rubric (headwords, numerals, key terms)   | warm red                                               | crimson                                 |

- Blue is the thread through both schemes: lapis at night, cobalt by day.
- Two of the colour names are themselves Arabic or Persian: Azur from _lāzaward_ (lapis), Karmesin from _qirmiz_ (the kermes insect).
- Purple is not used as a main colour. It is the stock colour of AI start-ups.
- The gold and the parchment tone continue from the previous version of the site, so the brand keeps a link to what was there.
- The exact values live in `src/styles.css` (section 11).

### 5.2 Type

- **EB Garamond** for all Latin and Greek text. It revives Claude Garamont's types after the Egenolff-Berner specimen (Frankfurt am Main, 1592) and includes polytonic Greek and the phonetic alphabet.
- **Amiri** for Arabic. It revives the naskh typeface of the Bulaq Press in Cairo (1905).
- Both are open source and are served from the site itself. No font is loaded from a third party, which also removes a GDPR problem the old site had.
- No sans-serif and no monospace. Labels are set in small, letter-spaced capitals of the text face. The font as shipped has no small-caps feature, so `font-variant-caps` must not be used.
- Dictionary conventions: bold headword, italic labels, angle brackets for etymology.

### 5.3 Layout

- Two columns on wide screens: main text and margin notes. One column on small screens, with each note after its paragraph.
- A margin note opens with its keyword in red small caps, then the text. No footnote numbers.
- Sections are separated by a hairline with the eight-pointed star.
- Corners are square throughout.

### 5.4 Motion

- The vault is the only animated element. The figure stays pinned while the visitor scrolls past it, and over that distance the plan rises into the vault. It also turns very slowly.
- Visitors who prefer reduced motion get the finished vault as a still image.

## 6. Voice

- German, formal address ("Sie") everywhere.
- Short declarative sentences. No superlatives and no promises that cannot be kept (the old "ohne Halluzinationen" is gone).
- Say what does not work as readily as what does.
- Margin notes are written for a curious general reader. Names get dates on first mention. No untranslated technical term.

### Transliteration

- Simplified scholarly transliteration with macrons, ʿ for ʿayn and ʾ for hamza: al-Rāzī, al-Sanūsī, Ibn Khaldūn, al-Khwārizmī, al-Jazarī, kalām, ḥāshiya.
- The article is always written "al-" (al-Rāzī, not ar-Rāzī).
- Latinised names are added on first mention where a German reader knows them: Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna), Ibn Rushd (Averroes).

## 8. Left out on purpose

- The Mechanical Turk as emblem or image (4.10).
- Tyrian purple as a main colour (5.1).
- Square Kufic or any calligraphic logo. It would read as decoration.
- The Rub el Hizb sign (۞). It is a Qurʾānic division mark.
- Sigrid Hunke's _Allahs Sonne über dem Abendland_ as a reference. The author's Nazi past disqualifies it.
- The claim that al-Jazarī's elephant clock represents five civilisations. It is widely repeated but not securely sourced to his text.

## 9. Ideas not yet used

- Kalām atomism and discrete time (the accident that does not last two moments) as an image for token-by-token generation.
- The theory of grammatical "agents" (_ʿawāmil_), after al-Jurjānī's _Hundred Regents_.
- The rules of disputation (_ādāb al-baḥth_) as an ancestor of multi-agent debate protocols.
- Al-Ṣūlī's chess problem from 10th-century Baghdad, unsolved for a thousand years, as an early "benchmark".
- Ibn Khaldūn was born in Tunis, which ties him to the Sidi Bou Said palette.

### 11.3 Adding a margin note

1. Record the tie in section 4 of this document, with its source and its status in section 10.
2. Put a `<Gloss term="…">` into the `notes` of the `Row` it belongs to. The term is one German word that also makes sense as a keyword for the text beside it.
3. Write Arabic with `<Ar>`, followed by `<Tr>` for the transliteration and a German meaning. Write Greek with `<Gr>`.
4. Keep it under about 70 words.

### 11.4 The vault's parameters

`VAULT` in `muqarnas/geometry.ts` sets the shape: 16 points per ring, 7 tiers, each ring 0.77 times the radius of the one below, pointed arches on short upright stilts. `Muqarnas.tsx` sets the view: how far the vault tilts and how strong the perspective is when it has fully risen. The server renders the ground plan as SVG, so the plan is on the page before any script runs; the canvas takes over once the script has loaded.

### 11.5 Limits of the typeface

- EB Garamond as shipped by Fontsource has no small-caps and no old-style-figures feature. Labels use capitals at a smaller size instead.
- It has no ⟨ ⟩ and no arrows. Use ‹ › for etymology brackets and do not use arrows in text.
